\section{Introduction}
For a simple/ideal spring, the  magnitude of the force it applies when being stretched is observed to be proportional to the stretched distance , and with direction opposite from the direction it is being pulled. Which, let proportionality constant be denoted as $k>0$, the associated differential equation is given as $F = m \ddot x = -k x$, which represents an equation for a Simple Harmonic Oscillator (SHO). As a result, the idealized spring as an SHO would have its period provided as $\tau=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$ after solving the differential equation.

\hfill

The experiment is to measure the spring constant of a spring provided in the lab. In addition, it'll also be focusing on the static relation between the mass and the spring stretch of a spring, together with how variation of mass and maximum amplitude would affect the time period of a spring's dynamic motion.

\section{Methods of Measurement and Quantities to Measure}
In all the experiments, for any pairs of manipulated variables, a total of $3$ trials were implemented. Following subsections characterize what is measured in each type of the experiment (either the static or the dynamic approach).
\subsection{Static Approach}
For the static approach, the aim is to measure the change in the stretch distance of the spring, in response to the change of the mass on the spring.

Here, the mass will be measured using a mass scale (unit: kg), and the stretch of the spring will be measured through the meter stick (unit: m). Which, the massess being tested are given by $n \cdot 50$ g (where integer $n$ satisfies $1\leq n\leq 10$). In terms of kg, it's given by $n \cdot 0.05$ kg, for every integer $n$ satisfying $1\leq n\leq 10$.

\subsection{Dynamic Approach}
For the dynamic approach, the aim is to measure the time period for the spring (a SHO) to complete a cycle, and to observe the change in the oscillator's period when varying the mass 

The mass measurement would again be done by a mass scale (unit: kg), and the maximum amplitude of the oscillation will be measured through the meter stick (unit: m). The time period measurement then differ depending on the experiment:
\begin{itemize}
    \item For the experiment recording data manually, a timer is used to measure the time period (unit: s).
    \item For the experiment recording data with detector, the detector served as a timer for measurement.
\end{itemize}
In both experiments about the spring's dynamical system, the spring oscillated in total of $5$ times, and record the total time elapsed for the five cycles to get the averge time period for the spring.

However, the amplitudes used differ by experiments: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item For the experiment recording data manually, the proposed maximum amplitudes are $2,4,6$ cm respectively (or $0.02,0.04,0.06$ m).
    \item For the experiment recording data with detector, the maxium amplitude is fixed as $2$ cm ($0.02$ m).
\end{itemize}

For the masses that are used, the list differed by the experiments:
\begin{itemize}
    \item For the experiment measuring time manually, the masses are $100,200,300$ g respectively (or $0.1,0.2,0.3$ kg respectively).
    \item For the experiment measuring time with detector, the masses are $100,150,200$ g respectively (or $0.1,0.15,0.2$ kg).
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Procedure}
\subsection{Static Approach}
The Static Approach followed the procedure below:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[1.] Measure the initial static equilibrium point of the spring's free end using the meter stick (where meter stick should be fixed at the same height across the whole experiment).
    \item[2.] Fix a mass that's going to be tested, put the mass on the hook, and hang the hook on the free end of the spring.
    \item[3.] Wait till the spring system be at static equilibrium, then measure the new static equilibrium point of the spring's free end.
    \item[4.] Record the difference of this new position away from the initial position recorded in Step 1. 
    \item[5.] Repeat Step 2 to 4 in total of three trials.
    \item[6.] Change the mass to other ones awaited to be tested, and repeat Step 2 to 5 for each mass.  
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Remark:} any mass with the form $n\cdot 50$ g (where integer $1\leq n\leq 10$) can be formed using two $50$ g, two $100$ g, and one $200$ g, so the provided masses can complete the experiment.

\subsection{Dynamic Approach}
\subsubsection{Experiment without Detector}
The Dynamic Approach (without detector) followed the procedure below:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[1.] Fix a mass that is being used, put the mass onto the hook, and hang it onto the spring until it reaches sattic equilibrium.
    \item[2.] Fix a maximum amplitude that is being used (say $l$), pull the spring down until it is distance $l$ away from the equilibrium point.
    \item[3.] Release the spring, and start the timer simulteneously. Wait until the spring oscillates total of $5$ cycles and record the time it takes to complete it.
    \item[4.] Repeat Step 2 to 3 for a total of three trials. 
    \item[5.] Change the maximum amplitude to other values await to be tested, and repeat Step 2 to 4 for each desired maximum amplitude.
    \item[6.] Change the mass to other values await to be tested, and repeat Step 2 to 5 for each mass.   
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Experiment with Detector}
For the case with detector, it merely follows the procedure used in \textbf{Section 4.2.2}, except the timer's measurement is swapped using the detector.

\textbf{Remark:} When measuring using the detector, need to make sure when starting the measurement, the detector is right on the equilibrium point. Since when the mass varies, the equilibrium point of the spring would change accordingly, need to adjust the height of the detector through fixing the clamp at the corresponding height.

Here, the data and graph under static approach is as expected: It reflects a highly precise linearity between the mass (which is proportioanl to the gravitational force), and the spring's stretch. 

Since when the spring is at equilibrium after putting the mass on, the spring force should cancel the gravitational force, hence the spring force has the same magnitude as the gravitational force, which is proportional to mass. Here, the data has a high confidence of relfecting such property, due to the RMSD value being all $0.001$ (after rounding to $3$ significant figures).

Now, to derive a more precise relation between the mass on the spring and the stretch length of the spring, using a log-log plot it becomes the following graph:

Which, the slope of the trendline of the log-log plot is $1.03$. If given that stretch length $l = A m^b$ (where $A,b in \RR$ are constants yet to be determined), then $\log(l)=\log(A)+b\log(m)$, so the slope of the log-log plot represents the exponent $b$ in this case. Which, at each of the data point, since the original uncertainty $\delta l \approx 0.001$ (given in Table \ref{tab:data_static}), then the propogation of uncertainty on $log(l)$ is given by $\delta \log(l)=\frac{\partial log(l)}{\partial l}\delta l = \frac{\delta l}{l \cdot \ln(10)}$. \section{Calculation of Spring Constant $k$}
Recall that for the spring to be at equilibrium, the spring force needs to cancel out the gravitational force, hence the two in theory have the same magnitude. Hence, let $F_s$ denotes the spring force, $F_s = mg$; moreover, with the model that an ideal spring has the spring force being linearly proportional to the stretch length, $F_s = k x$, where $k$ is the spring constant. Hence, $k x = mg$, which implies $k = \frac{mg}{x}$ (unit: N/m). 

Similarly, since the period of the ideal spring (as SHO) is given by $\tau=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$, hence $k = \frac{4\pi^2 m}{\tau^2}$ (unit: N/m).

\section{Error Analysis and Potential Improvement}
For all the experiments, disregarding the human mistakes or errors made during measurement (due to the limitation of human eyes / reaction time), here are some potential sources of errors in the experiment:
\begin{itemize}
    \item First, the Spring is not ideal: It has nontrivial mass that potentially affects the stretch of the spring.
    \item Then, the spring's stretch is also affected after each trial: Every time the spring is being stretched, there is potentially some implicit change / damage in the structure of the spring that is not obvious. Such damage could potentially cause the spring constant to vary.
    \item During the dynamic approach, friction and air resistance are some of the causes of the damping force on the spring, which the spring is not a perfect Simple Harmonic Oscillator. So, the measured period would be different from the theoretical calculation.
    \item During the dynamic approach with detector, it is observed multiple times that the detector is not as consistent for the measurement, which there is a possibility that the recorded data includes some non-negligible errors.
\end{itemize}
In terms of improvement, some of the following can be made to the procedure or the actual implementation of the experiment:
\begin{itemize}
    \item To prevent the spring from having nontrivial change / damage to its structure, could consider put an upper bound on the masses that are being measured to decrease the force applied on the spring each time.
    \item For the dynamic approach without detector, for a more accurate measurement, one can again consider using the camera to record the whole oscillation for a more precise time measurement.
\end{itemize}
